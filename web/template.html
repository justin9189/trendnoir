What counts as reasonable?

Every window of years that is at least ten years long and covers at least half the record, analyzed four defensible ways: the main test, Hamed and Rao’s stricter correction, the seasonal Kendall test, and a test on annual means. Short windows can’t see small trends, so we also report windows long enough to catch a trend of this size.

How the detective thinks

Five ideas behind every verdict. The first four are live: move the sliders and drag the points, and the statistics respond.

1

Seasons hide slow change

A yearly cycle can be far bigger than a decades-long trend. Comparing each month with its own normal value removes the cycle and leaves the trend in plain sight.

The made-up record on the right rises by 0.8 units per decade, whatever the size of the seasons.

2

A slope that ignores troublemakers

The usual straight-line fit, least squares, lets one extreme month pull the whole line. Sen’s slope draws a line through every pair of points and takes the middle slope, so a single troublemaker barely moves it.

Drag the red point up or down.

3

A trend test that counts votes

The Mann-Kendall test compares every pair of points: does the later one come out higher or lower? With no trend, that is a coin flip, so ups and downs split about evenly. A trend tilts the vote.

later point higherlater point lower
4

Months that remember

A cold month tends to be followed by another cold month. Records with memory wander in long swings that look like trends. A plain t-test treats every month as fresh evidence and is fooled; the careful test counts how much independent evidence the record really holds.

Every record simulated here has no trend at all, so a fair test should cry wolf about 5% of the time.

One analysis can be lucky

Pick your years after looking at the data and you can find almost any trend. So TrendNoir never trusts one window. It maps every window of years and runs every reasonable analysis, then reports how many agree.

Words in one sentence

p-value
How often luck alone would produce a pattern at least this strong. Small means luck is an unlikely explanation.
Significant
By convention, p below 0.05. It says the pattern is unlikely to be luck, not that it is large or important.
Trend
The steady part of a change across a window of years, given here as change per decade.
Seasonal cycle
The pattern that repeats every year. Removed by subtracting each calendar month’s long-term average.
Sen’s slope
The middle slope among all pairs of points. A few extreme values can’t drag it around.
Mann-Kendall test
Counts whether later values tend to beat earlier ones. With no trend, it’s a coin flip.
Autocorrelation
Memory: neighboring months resemble each other, so they carry less independent information than their number suggests.
Effective sample size
How many truly independent values a record is worth once its memory is accounted for.
Cherry-picking
Choosing start and end years after seeing the data, to get the answer you want.
Multiverse analysis
Running every reasonable version of an analysis and reporting how many agree.

How the verdicts are reached

The routine

  1. Average every measurement into monthly values. Months with fewer than ten valid satellite days would be dropped; none were.
  2. Remove the seasons by subtracting each calendar month’s long-term average.
  3. Measure the change with Sen’s slope: the median slope over every pair of months.
  4. Test it with the Mann-Kendall test, which asks how often a later month beats an earlier one. With no trend that would be a coin flip.
  5. Because neighboring months resemble each other, shrink the sample to an effective size using the lag-1 autocorrelation, then recompute the p-value and the range.
  6. Get two second opinions. Hamed and Rao’s stricter correction (1998) uses every significant lag of memory, not just one. The seasonal Kendall test with Hirsch and Slack’s correction (1984) works on the raw record and compares each calendar month only with itself.
  7. Find the turning point, if there is one: fit a line with a single bend at every possible month, keep the best, and estimate how sure we are of the bend with a block bootstrap that keeps the data’s memory.
  8. Repeat the main test for every window of eight or more years to draw the map.
  9. Suggest variables with a fixed rule table, not AI: each kind of place and interest maps to measurements from MODIS, OMI, AIRS, GPM, MERRA-2 and GLDAS, with a plain definition and a reason a trend might show there.
  10. Score robustness with a multiverse analysis (Steegen and colleagues, 2016): every window of at least ten years and at least half the record, analyzed four defensible ways (the main test, Hamed and Rao, seasonal Kendall, and annual means). Because short windows lack the power to see small trends, we also report the result for windows long enough to catch a trend of that size.
  11. Measure false alarms: chop the record’s trend-free residuals into blocks long enough to keep their memory, shuffle the blocks into 400 fake records, and count how often each test calls them significant.

The “careless” p-value on the map is an ordinary straight-line fit that ignores month-to-month memory. It is what many first attempts use, and it is shown here as the thing to distrust.

“How long” uses the rule of thumb from Weatherhead and colleagues (1998): the years needed to catch a trend nine times out of ten depend on the noise, its memory and the size of the trend.

What to keep in mind

  • Straight-line trends only, as the challenge asks. A curve, like China’s rise and fall, is described by splitting the record, not modeled.
  • The main memory correction uses only the first lag. The stricter Hamed and Rao version agrees with it on all nine records here.
  • Two popular shortcuts failed our checks, so we left them out. On land snow, trend-free pre-whitening and the uncorrected seasonal Kendall test gave p-values of 0.000004 and 0.00000001, against 0.26 from the careful test. Both treat strongly remembered noise as fresh evidence.
  • The month-by-month view runs twelve tests, so about one month could look significant by chance.
  • Hundreds of windows are tested on the map. Their p-values are not corrected for that, and a window chosen after looking is not a fair test. That is the point of the exercise.
  • MERRA-2 values are reanalysis: a model steered by observations, not direct measurements. Soil wetness and aerosols in particular depend on the model.
  • The OMI satellite’s coverage changed after 2007 because of a sensor problem known as the row anomaly. We did not adjust for it.
  • “No trend detected” means no trend larger than the stated size, not proof that nothing ever changes.
  • The case files are computed in Python before the page loads. Uploaded files are analyzed in your browser with a JavaScript copy of the same methods, which we checked against Python on every raw Giovanni file used here. No AI model produces or alters any number.
