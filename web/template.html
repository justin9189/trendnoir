NASA has decades of Earth measurements. Most are noisy. TrendNoir walks any one of them through four steps and gives you a single plain sentence: believe it, doubt it, or wait for more data.

The case files

Every case follows the same routine. Pull the record, remove the seasons, measure the change, test the evidence. Then try your best to break the result.

Try to break it

A trend can appear, vanish or flip depending on which years you pick. Drag the window, or click a square on the map. Each square is one possible choice of first and last year. Stronger color means a steeper slope; a dot means the change passes the careful test.

How hard is it to kill?

One analysis can be lucky. So we run every reasonable one: each window of years that is at least ten years long and covers at least half the record, times four defensible methods. Each dot below is one analysis, sorted by the slope it found. A trend worth believing survives most of them and is reversed by none.

Spot the real one

Nine records. Eight are pure noise, built from this record’s own wobble and month-to-month memory, with the trend taken out. One is the real measurement. Pick the one you think is real. If you can’t tell it apart from noise, be wary of anyone who says they can.

How long until it’s real?

When the evidence falls short, the honest next question is how much more data would settle it.

Bring your own evidence

Any of the roughly two thousand variables in NASA’s Giovanni portal, over any region you draw, can go through the same routine. Compare two regions to see whether the same measurement moves the same way in both places. Your files are read and analyzed inside your browser and never leave it.

Get a file from Giovanni

  1. Open giovanni.gsfc.nasa.gov and sign in with a free NASA Earthdata account.
  2. Set the plot type to Time Series, Area-Averaged.
  3. Pick a variable (monthly ones are quickest), draw your region and choose at least ten full years.
  4. Select Plot Data, then download the result as CSV. Daily data works too. If a long daily record times out, download it in parts and drop all the parts here together.

The sample is the raw Giovanni download behind Case 002. Its verdict should match the case file exactly.

How the verdicts are reached

The routine

  1. Average every measurement into monthly values. Months with fewer than ten valid satellite days would be dropped; none were.
  2. Remove the seasons by subtracting each calendar month’s long-term average.
  3. Measure the change with Sen’s slope: the median slope over every pair of months.
  4. Test it with the Mann-Kendall test, which asks how often a later month beats an earlier one. With no trend that would be a coin flip.
  5. Because neighboring months resemble each other, shrink the sample to an effective size using the lag-1 autocorrelation, then recompute the p-value and the range.
  6. Get two second opinions. Hamed and Rao’s stricter correction (1998) uses every significant lag of memory, not just one. The seasonal Kendall test with Hirsch and Slack’s correction (1984) works on the raw record and compares each calendar month only with itself.
  7. Find the turning point, if there is one: fit a line with a single bend at every possible month, keep the best, and estimate how sure we are of the bend with a block bootstrap that keeps the data’s memory.
  8. Repeat the main test for every window of eight or more years to draw the map.
  9. Score robustness with a multiverse analysis (Steegen and colleagues, 2016): every window of at least ten years and at least half the record, analyzed four defensible ways (the main test, Hamed and Rao, seasonal Kendall, and annual means). Because short windows lack the power to see small trends, we also report the result for windows long enough to catch a trend of that size.
  10. Build the noise lineup: chop the record’s trend-free residuals into blocks long enough to keep their memory, shuffle the blocks, and deal eight fake records. This is the “lineup” method of visual inference from Buja and colleagues (2009).

The “careless” p-value on the map is an ordinary straight-line fit that ignores month-to-month memory. It is what many first attempts use, and it is shown here as the thing to distrust.

“How long” uses the rule of thumb from Weatherhead and colleagues (1998): the years needed to catch a trend nine times out of ten depend on the noise, its memory and the size of the trend.

What to keep in mind

  • Straight-line trends only, as the challenge asks. A curve, like China’s rise and fall, is described by splitting the record, not modeled.
  • The main memory correction uses only the first lag. The stricter Hamed and Rao version agrees with it on all nine records here.
  • Two popular shortcuts failed our checks, so we left them out. On land snow, trend-free pre-whitening and the uncorrected seasonal Kendall test gave p-values of 0.000004 and 0.00000001, against 0.26 from the careful test. Both treat strongly remembered noise as fresh evidence.
  • The month-by-month view runs twelve tests, so about one month could look significant by chance.
  • Hundreds of windows are tested on the map. Their p-values are not corrected for that, and a window chosen after looking is not a fair test. That is the point of the exercise.
  • MERRA-2 values are reanalysis: a model steered by observations, not direct measurements. Soil wetness and aerosols in particular depend on the model.
  • The OMI satellite’s coverage changed after 2007 because of a sensor problem known as the row anomaly. We did not adjust for it.
  • “No trend detected” means no trend larger than the stated size, not proof that nothing ever changes.
  • The case files are computed in Python before the page loads. Uploaded files and the noise lineup are analyzed in your browser with a JavaScript copy of the same methods, which we checked against Python on every raw Giovanni file used here. No AI model produces or alters any number.
